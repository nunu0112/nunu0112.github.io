Research/Joint behavioral–neural modeling

Joint Modeling of Single-Trial Behavior and fMRI Activity

A factor-analytic neural diffusion decision model with a Bayesian lasso linking function, extended to a rewarded recognition-memory task so that decision parameters and brain responses are estimated together rather than in sequence.

Authors Eunwoo Kim*, Seoyoung Choi*, Sungwoo An, Sanghoon Han, & Inhan Kang (*equal contribution)
Status Analysis in progress
Role Co-first author; modeling and estimation
Presented CBrain 2026, poster

Abstract

Most previous applications of computational modeling to neuroscience first estimate cognitive model components and then relate them to measured neural activity. This two-step analysis has two limitations: uncertainty in the cognitive parameter estimates is not propagated to subsequent analyses, and neural data cannot inform estimation of the cognitive structure. The joint modeling framework in model-based cognitive neuroscience (MBCN; Turner et al., 2017) instead simultaneously estimates submodels for behavioral and neural data, as well as a linking function between their parameters. This approach allows the two modalities to constrain each other and treat associations between cognitive components and brain regions as quantities estimated within the same model rather than as post-hoc correlations. We demonstrate this framework with the fMRI word recognition data of Han et al. (2010), in which reward contingencies for recognition judgments were manipulated. The diffusion decision model (DDM) accounts for behavior, decomposing choices and response times into trial-wise information-processing rates and other cognitive components. The neural side uses single-trial beta estimates from regions of interest (ROIs) defined by the AAL3 atlas. A factor-analytic structure with Lasso regularization links the two sets of parameters and yields coefficients representing relationships between cognitive components and ROIs. For illustrative purposes, we apply this model to single-participant data with multiple experimental conditions and explore patterns of single-trial DDM parameters and their associations with ROIs across conditions. This application demonstrates how the framework jointly addresses the multimodal nature of data and brain-behavior connections within a unified model.

Background

Model-based cognitive neuroscience exists to give latent model components — the rate at which evidence accumulates, the caution with which a threshold is set — a neural interpretation rather than leaving them as descriptive fits to behavior. In practice this is almost always done in two steps: fit a cognitive model to behavior, take the resulting parameter estimates, and relate them to brain activity.

That two-step approach has two specific costs.

  • Parameter uncertainty is not propagated Carrying point estimates into a second-stage correlation treats a noisy quantity as if it were known. This bites hardest for single-trial parameters, which are noisy by construction, and the consequence is attenuation — the true brain–behavior association is systematically underestimated.
  • Information flows only one way The neural data never get to say anything back about the cognitive model. They are a destination, not a constraint.
The alternative. The joint modeling framework in model-based cognitive neuroscience (Turner et al., 2017) specifies a behavioral submodel, a neural submodel, and a linking function between their parameters, and estimates all three simultaneously. Uncertainty propagates correctly, the neural data help identify the cognitive parameters, and brain–behavior associations become quantities estimated inside the model rather than post-hoc correlations.

Methods

Task and data

  • Design Old/new word recognition with fMRI, 20 participants (11 female, mean age 24). Three blocked reward conditions: no reward (200 trials), hit reward (100 trials; +$1 for a hit, −$1 for a false alarm), and correct-rejection reward (100 trials; +$1 for a correct rejection, −$1 for a miss).
  • Neural preprocessing Slice-timing correction, realignment, and MNI normalization; single-trial beta estimation by Least Squares Separate (Mumford et al., 2012), which fits one GLM per trial so that trial-level activity estimates stay stable when trials are closely spaced; conversion to percent signal change; and averaging within AAL3 atlas parcels to give one value per trial per region.
  • Alignment Behavioral and neural rows are reconciled explicitly — run order was counterbalanced across participants, so physical run index and condition label do not coincide. Trials dropped on one side are dropped on the other, with a hard row-count check and a rank-correlation check between behavioral and neural trial onsets.

The three submodels

  • Behavioral — diffusion decision model (RT_t, r_t) ~ Wiener(a, τ_t, ζ_t, ξ_t). Boundary separation a is a single policy parameter, while non-decision time τ_t, starting point ζ_t, and drift rate ξ_t vary trial to trial — these three are exactly the quantities that become latent factors. Positivity and boundedness are handled by estimating τ on the log scale and ζ on the logit scale, and drift is signed by stimulus type so that β_stim estimates old–new drift separation as a model-based discriminability index.
  • Neural Trial-level, ROI-averaged percent-signal-change betas as p observed indicators with a multivariate normal measurement model and region-specific residual variances Ψ.
  • Linking function A factor-analytic structure in which the three trial-level decision parameters are the latent factors, and ROI responses load onto them through a p × 3 matrix Λ. Each Λ_jk is the strength with which region j tracks cognitive component k, so Σ = ΛΦΛᵀ + Ψ.

What makes the model genuinely joint is that the decision parameters and the neural signal are drawn from one (3+p) × (3+p) covariance structure, rather than from two models linked afterwards. The neural data therefore help identify the single-trial decision parameters, and the uncertainty in those parameters propagates into the loadings.

Regularization and identification

  • Bayesian lasso on the loadings A hierarchical normal–exponential scale mixture — Λ_jk ~ Normal(0, √(τ²_jk ψ_j)) with τ²_jk ~ Exponential(κ²/2) and κ² ~ Gamma(5, 0.05) — shrinking irrelevant loadings toward zero. With well over a hundred AAL3 regions this is not optional.
  • Factor covariance Φ parameterized through a Cholesky factor with an LKJ(2) prior on the correlation matrix and half-Cauchy scales.
  • Rotational indeterminacy Resolved with sign-constrained anchor regions — one designated ROI per factor with a positive loading floor, and cross-loadings parameterized as bounded ratios of the anchor loading. Without this the factors are only identified up to rotation and the loading matrix is uninterpretable.
  • Condition comparison inside one model A variant with condition-specific loadings Λ_c and shrinkage-prior mean offsets lets the three reward conditions be compared within a single fit instead of by fitting three separate models and comparing point estimates.

Estimation

  • Sampler Stan via rstan, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with NUTS, using Stan's built-in first-passage-time density for the diffusion likelihood.
  • Two-stage fitting An initial run of 6 chains × 1,000 iterations with hand-constructed informative starting values; the chain with the highest mean log-posterior is then used to seed the main run of 3 chains × 10,000 iterations (2,000 warm-up), adapt_delta 0.85. This is a practical answer to a hard geometry: naive initialization on a model with this many trial-level parameters wastes most of the sampling budget.
  • Scale Models are pre-compiled and run as SLURM array jobs over the participant × condition grid.
  • Diagnostics R̂, effective sample size, divergent-transition counts, and trace plots for the log-posterior and the anchor loadings, saved with every fit and summarized across participants.
  • Validation Parameter-recovery datasets were simulated across noise levels and loading structures at both p = 30 and p = 128 before the model was applied to real data. Three linking structures (free, independent, and unit-variance factor covariance) are implemented and compared.

Results

Results below are from a single participant; the group-level analysis is in progress.

One result is worth reading carefully because it looks backwards. Under correct-rejection reward the starting point leaned toward "old", even though that condition pays for saying "new". The resolution is that the penalized error in that condition is a miss, so leaning "old" is the safe default when evidence is ambiguous — the model recovers a bias that tracks the penalty structure rather than the reward structure.

An honest caveat. For this participant the two rewarded conditions ran in a fixed order, so reward condition is confounded with fatigue. Separating them is exactly what the counterbalanced group-level analysis is for, and is the reason these results are presented as an illustration of the framework rather than as a substantive finding.
