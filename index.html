About

I build and validate formal models of the processes that generate psychological data. My work sits at the intersection of cognitive modeling, model-based cognitive neuroscience, and interpretable machine learning — chosen in each case by the mechanism I am trying to recover, not by allegiance to a single toolkit.

Three commitments run through my projects. First, a model should explain more than one channel of data at a time: choices and response times arise from the same decision process, and neural activity is informative about that process rather than a separate outcome to be correlated with it afterwards. Second, individual-level estimates are only useful if they can be trusted, which makes parameter recovery, posterior predictive checking, and honest model comparison part of the contribution rather than an appendix. Third, flexibility and interpretability are not opposed: where the structure of a problem is unknown, flexible learners paired with principled attribution methods can reveal it.

Concretely, I have joined reinforcement learning with sequential sampling models so that a single mechanism accounts for what people choose and how long they take; extended a factor-analytic neural diffusion decision model so that single-trial brain responses and decision parameters are estimated together; and used tree-based ensembles with Shapley attribution and causal forests to study questions where the relevant structure is nonlinear and heterogeneous. I work primarily in Stan, R, and Python, with formal training in computer science supporting the implementation side of this work.
